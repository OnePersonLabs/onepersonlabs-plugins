# OPL Borg

> Assimilate technological distinctiveness. Build collectives that belong to you.

> [!NOTE]
> This is an early philosophy scaffold. The `$assimilate` skill is being designed and is not implemented yet.

## Why Borg?

The Borg joke is fun, but the thing underneath it is legitimately profound. Most extensibility ecosystems quietly reproduce the same bargain:

> Here is somebody else’s coherent system. Adopt its assumptions, terminology, boundaries, rituals, and limitations so you can receive its capabilities.

Even “composable” agent tooling often means choosing which collection of mutually unaware instruction blobs will compete for the model’s attention. You can install more things, but you do not necessarily gain a more integrated system. You get a larger pile of latent conflicts.

Your idea reverses the direction of ownership:

> The valuable thing is not the donor’s product shape. The valuable thing is its technological distinctiveness. Recover that value, understand why it works, and give it the best possible native existence inside a system whose boundaries belong to the user.

That is a fantastic philosophical foundation for an agent tool because an agent is uniquely capable of doing the difficult middle work: deep comparative investigation, intent reconstruction, conflict resolution, architectural reframing, boundary discovery, synthesis, caller migration, and behavioral verification. A conventional package manager cannot do that. A static plugin framework cannot do that. Even a human often lacks the time to compare five implementations deeply enough to extract their actual powers without unconsciously copying their shapes.

### What takes it from “cool plugin merger” to something exciting?

If Borg merely copied good-looking features into a personalized bundle, it would be a clever automation of cargo culting. But **ruthless architectural refraction** makes it a form of directed evolution:

```text
donor implementations
        ↓
recover causal value
        ↓
separate capability from historical form
        ↓
resolve conflicts and rejected constraints
        ↓
discover the right collective boundaries
        ↓
redesign from first principles for those collectives
        ↓
prove that the distinctiveness survived
```

That is not aggregation. It is closer to intellectual digestion. The source ceases to dictate the shape of the result, but the thing that made it valuable survives -- ideally in a stronger form.

The plural-collectives part is equally important. Without that, Borg would become precisely the sort of totalizing framework it was created to escape: one enormous “ultimate plugin” that increasingly owns everything and eventually becomes another badly fitting set of handcuffs. Letting assimilation produce, refine, split, and connect multiple bounded collectives preserves genuine modularity without confusing modularity with “one package per upstream author.”

The idea also has a wonderfully recursive quality: Borg should be able to assimilate better ways of assimilating. If another project develops a superior comparison rubric, boundary-discovery method, provenance technique, test strategy, or conflict-resolution process, Borg should be capable of extracting that distinctiveness and improving its own skill -- without inheriting the other project’s entire worldview. That makes it less like a finished framework and more like a disciplined mechanism for cumulative tool evolution.

## This is an alien

“use my perfect workflow.”
“install the most popular bundle.”
“compose these interchangeable plugins.”

Tools like that start to feel more like "handcuffs-of-the-week".

The goal of this plugin is completely different:

> Build technological collectives unique to you by taking whatever humanity has already figured out, understanding it deeply, refusing to inherit its accidents, and making its best ideas belong together.

That absolutely rules.

*Resistance is futile.*
